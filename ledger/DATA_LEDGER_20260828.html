1. Introduction

A mixture-of-experts (MoE) layer routes each token to a few of many expert blocks. The standard question is whether those experts divide up knowledge by subject. The best known answer, from the Mixtral report, is no. That answer was measured over supplied text, which the model processes in a single parallel pass (prefill). The model also routes tokens while it writes its answer, one token at a time (generation). Nobody had asked whether the two passes agree.

They do not. Over prefill, one expert wins nearly every subject. Over generation, each subject gets its own leading expert. The shift holds when prefill and generation are matched in length, and it repeats in a second, larger model whose expert numbering has nothing to do with the first. This ledger lists what was run, where each number lives, how much weight each number carries, and why the result matters.

2. What the experiments were

Three runs, all greedy decoding, all on 8-bit community fine-tunes of Qwen3.5 MoE models (HauhauCS "Uncensored Aggressive" variants), all captured with a patched llama.cpp binary that dumps router logits per token per layer. Routing weights are rebuilt the same way everywhere: softmax over 256 router logits, keep the top 8, renormalize to sum to one. For an expert, W is its mean weight over all tokens, S the fraction of tokens that select it, Q its mean weight when selected. A domain's winner is the expert with the highest W over that domain's prompts and all layers.

Run 1. 35B primary probe

60 short questions, 20 academic domains, 3 per domain. Qwen3.5-35B-A3B (40 layers, 256 experts, top-8, about 3B active). One capture per prompt, split at the prompt boundary: prompt tokens are the prefill block, produced tokens are the generation block. Generation capped at 2,056 tokens. The generation block was also analyzed twice, once trimmed at the first chat-template spill and once untrimmed.

Run 2. 35B token-balanced control

Same model. Three prompts, each packing twenty domain questions and padded to exactly 446 prompt tokens, each allowed 2,048 generated tokens. Purpose: check that the prefill/generation difference is not just a short-set versus long-set artifact. Compared the top-expert sets of the two passes (Jaccard overlap) and the per-token routing entropy of each pass.

Run 3. 122B replication

Same 60 prompts on Qwen3.5-122B-A10B (48 layers, 36 gated-DeltaNet and 12 softmax attention, 256 experts, top-8, about 10B active). Its expert indices are unrelated to the 35B model's. Two captures: a prefill-only pass (-n 0) and a generation pass (-n 2048). The generation pass also recorded its own prefill block.

5. Flags found in this audit

122B prefill is unpaired and not bit-stable. The paper's 122B prefill row (13/20, 7 distinct) comes from the routing-only run. The generation run captured its own prefill with the same binary hash, prompts, and flags, differing only in -n, and there E233 wins 15/20 with 6 distinct. The claim holds under either reading (both concentrated; generation 18 distinct regardless). But the paper's sentence that prefill routing "is deterministic given the prompt and the weights" is contradicted by this archive, and Table 1 pairs a prefill from one capture with a generation from another without saying so. Fix: report the paired 15/20 or keep 13/20 and disclose the separate capture and the gap.
E40 example cuts both ways. "Mid-pack in philosophy prefill" is true by W and false by the archive's composite score (rank 1). Either give the W rank or drop the example.
Smaller wording issues. "Each attending to everything before it" is wrong for the 30/40 and 36/48 gated-DeltaNet layers. Hayashi et al. use the official FP8 checkpoint, not "the same checkpoint we use." Per-token entropy (0.958) is never defined as top-8 normalized. The 35B .npz holds per-layer summaries, not raw router tensors.

6. Why it matters

The negative result was measured in the wrong pass. The most-cited evidence that MoE experts do not specialize by topic was taken over supplied text, which is prefill routing. Prefill routing in these models is dominated by one default expert regardless of subject. Read the same prompts over generation and each subject gets its own leader. So a prefill-only or pooled measurement understates specialization by construction, and a negative prefill result says nothing about generation.

It is a regime effect, not a length effect. With 446 prompt tokens against 2,048 generated tokens per chunk, the two passes still pick nearly disjoint expert sets, and per-token entropy barely moves. Generation spreads each token over about as many experts as prefill does and sends it to different ones. The leader identity changes; the spread does not. That is the specialization signal.

It replicates where it should. The 122B model has different depth, a hybrid DeltaNet/attention stack, and unrelated expert numbering. Its generalist is a different expert and its per-domain leaders are different experts, yet the concentrated-then-dispersed shape is the same. A second group, working on five unrelated models, independently found that prefill usage does not predict generation usage. The premise is not a quirk of one quantized fine-tune.

Practical consequence. Any expert-naming pipeline, specialization benchmark, or ablation study that scores routing over supplied text inherits the prefill picture. Measure the two passes separately and read specialization from generation. The XHIGH rows above are enough to carry that recommendation; the HIGH rows close the obvious objections; the flags are fixable in a revision without touching any XHIGH value.
