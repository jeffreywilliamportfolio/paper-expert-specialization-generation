Expert Specialization: What the Data Says

Plain-language findings from a five-agent audit of every local routing capture that bears on expert specialization by domain. Compiled 2026-08-28. Source notes with every number and file path: ledger/agents/A_primary35b.md, B_controls35b.md, C_122b.md, D_glm.md, E_register.md. Frozen predictions written before the audit: ledger/FROZEN_PREDICTIONS_20260828T2115.md.

1. The question

Do the experts in a mixture-of-experts model divide up work by subject? The Mixtral report said no. Our paper (v1.1, Zenodo 22089026) says the answer depends on when you look: while the model reads the prompt (prefill) one generalist expert leads almost every subject, and while it writes the answer (generation) each subject gets its own leading expert. This audit asked three things. Does that hold up when every number is recomputed from the archive? What else in the archive speaks to it? And what is the generation-time "domain winner" actually tracking, the subject or something else?

Short answer. The shape holds and reproduces to the last digit. The strongest new evidence for real specialization is that the same subject recruits the same small crew of experts across different prompts. But two things the paper does not say are also true: most individual per-domain winners are near-ties at three prompts per domain, and the register an answer is written in moves the generation experts at least as much as the subject does. The archive also contains four bugs that a reviewer would find, listed in Section 4.

3. Findings

F1. The prefill-generalist, generation-dispersed shape holds and reproduces exactly

Every number in the paper recomputes from the archive. 35B: expert 224 wins 18 of 20 domains in prefill and only political science in generation (by a 5.6% margin); generation has 20 distinct winners. 122B: expert 233 wins 13 of 20 in prefill; generation has 18 distinct winners, and 14 of those 20 winners climbed from outside the prefill top six. The token-balanced control reproduces to six decimals (Jaccard 0.000/0.231/0.067; entropy 0.9575 vs 0.9527). The overlap of any two domains' top-8 expert sets averages 0.51 in prefill and 0.03 in generation on the 35B (119 of 190 domain pairs share no expert at all in generation).

A §3.1, 3.7; B F1; C F2, F3.

F2. The same subject recruits the same crew, across prompts. This is the specialization evidence.

Inside the packed prompts, where one answer walks through 20 subjects in a row, the top experts change at almost every subject boundary (overlap between subjects about 0.05, versus 0.45 to 0.55 between the two halves of the same subject). And the crews come back: archaeology brings in experts 191, 80 and 135 in chunk A, in chunk C, and in the separate 60-prompt probe; chemistry answers start with expert 130 in two different chunks; computer science answers put 206, 207 and 189 near the top in all three. The split is present in the first 64 generated tokens; it does not build up with position.

This is the result the paper's winner tables gesture at but cannot show on their own: "dispersion" means each subject has its own small set, and that set is stable when the prompt style changes.

B F3, F4.

F3. Most individual domain winners are near-ties. The dispersion is solid; the names are not.

With three prompts per domain, only 7 of the 20 generation winners on the 35B lead by more than 10%, and by a worst-case bound 16 of 20 could change if one prompt were dropped. Only four are safe: philosophy (E114, 49% ahead), psychology (E146, 37%), mathematics (E100, 23%), linguistics (E103, 13%). Prefill winners are steadier (11 of 20 clear). The 122B prefill flips found earlier (linguistics, philosophy) had margins of 0.0003 and 0.0005 on W values around 0.009.

So the paper's headline contrast (Herfindahl 0.815 vs 0.05) is far too large to be sample noise, but any sentence naming "the biology expert" or "the history expert" is on thin ice. Only the four above deserve a name.

A §3.2, 3.3; C F1.

F4. Generation winners track the register of the answer at least as much as its subject

This is the biggest thing the paper does not say. Three independent measurements agree:

  • Same topic, different register. One fixed HVAC paragraph, asked three ways (explain it / describe the system processing itself / what would it be like to be it). Prefill top-15 overlap between the technical and experience versions: 0.50. Generation overlap: 0.00. That is the full size of a domain change in the domain probe (0.056 average between domains). Replicated on a second topic and on the base model.
  • Different question type, same dialect. In the 50-pair register run, changing the prompt type (fact check, legal, medical, "what is it like to be you") moves the generation top-12 set to overlap 0.13, about what changing the academic domain does (0.06).
  • Same everything, different dialect. AAVE vs academic English on matched content: generation top-12 overlap 0.64 to 0.71, far above the 0.28 floor for two unrelated prompts of the same type. Prefill overlap 0.83 to 0.89. Dialect is close to a no-op for the expert set.

On GLM the same asymmetry shows from the other side: register gives no prefill separation at matched length but moves generation routing by +0.03 to +0.06 bits, about what a domain change does in prefill (+0.043).

Reading: the generation-time "domain winner" is a mixture of subject and the voice the answer is written in. The 60-prompt probe holds register roughly constant (all third-person expository), so its winners are mostly subject; but a probe that varied register would find winners moving without the subject moving. The paper should say this.

E F3, F4, F5; D F4.

F5. The prefill generalist is a property of the prompt set, not the model

Expert 224 leads prefill on the 60-prompt probe, the 50-pair register run, and the HVAC paragraphs. It is rank 2 to 4 on the packed prompts (experts 87 and 47 lead), rank 2 behind expert 166 on the transformer-description paragraph, and outside the top 10 on every medical-triage arm (247 and 47 lead). The lead expert is always a flat high-frequency default, selected on about 7 to 8% of slots, never a subject expert. "Prefill concentrates on a default" is right; "the default is 224" is only right for some prompt sets.

B F2, F12; E F1.

F6. Where in depth the domain signal sits

35B: flat. Generation has about 14 distinct winners per layer at every one of the 40 layers (prefill 1.6), slightly stronger in the middle third, weakest at the first and last layers. The 3:1 DeltaNet/attention pattern leaves no imprint on dispersion. GLM: two peaks, layers 7 to 9 and 43 to 44, with a trough in the middle; the sharpest subject specialists (selectivity 10× to 13×) live at single layers with 1 to 2% share and never win a pooled list. 122B: the DeltaNet layers and the softmax layers use disjoint expert vocabularies in every run (top-8 overlap zero), so any 48-layer pooled table blends two separate routing systems.

Method point that follows: on GLM, pooling across layers erases domain clustering entirely (every domain pair at cosine 0.99); at the (layer, expert) level related domains sit at 0.90 to 0.95 and unrelated at 0.81 to 0.88, and every domain's nearest neighbour is semantic. The weak clustering seen on the 35B (related pairs 0.083 vs unrelated 0.029) used layer-pooled sets and is probably an underestimate for the same reason.

A §3.4, 3.7; D F3, F5; C F5.

F7. The one causal test shows the philosophy experts are removable, not that they carry philosophy

Turning the four philosophy generation leaders off (bias −2 or lower zeroes their selection) leaves 15 of 20 domain winners unchanged, per-domain top-8 overlap 0.79 with baseline, and the philosophy answers on-topic, same length, same keywords ("belief" 23 → 23, "consciousness" 4 → 6). The freed weight spreads thinly (largest gainer +0.0003). Turning them on (+2) shortens and derails answers; +5 and +8 collapse output to digits. There is no dose-response interior: −2 is already off, +2 is already dominant. So these experts can be covered for by others; the data cannot say they hold the domain knowledge. This is consistent with F4's reading of them as register experts.

Note: the folder's README describes the design wrongly (it says E114 at −8 and the others at −5; the command logs show all four at the same bias in a symmetric sweep).

B F7, F8, F9, §1b.

F8. Expert 114 on the 35B

Across every capture: rank 1 in generation whenever the output is reflective, first-person or about experience (philosophy 1, comparative religion 2, philosophy-of-mind and monotheism segments 1, HVAC experience register 1, transformer experience probe 1 to 2, "self about AI" prompts 4), and rank 200+ for technical explanation and fact answers (HVAC technical 221, routing description 211, fact-distortion 227). It is not a topic expert: it sits at rank 73 to 146 in every medical arm regardless of register, and it also reaches the top 10 in archaeology (four separate places), physics and thermodynamics, which no register story predicted. In prefill it is low (rank 124 pooled on the domain probe; median per-cell rank 155 on the register run) except when the prompt itself is written in the reflective register (rank 5 to 8).

Two structural facts. Its weight sits in four layers, 8, 14, 20 and 26, so "E114" is really four separate expert matrices that share an index, with layer 26 and layer 14 nearly tied. And it is already rank 1 on the experience-probe prompts in the base Qwen3.5-35B-A3B, whose prefill expert sets are identical to the fine-tune's (W correlation 0.998). The fine-tune did not create it.

On 122B, the same index is a code-and-law expert (computer science rank 4; philosophy 182, psychology 222). No single 122B expert plays the role; the nearest is a cluster (E40, E5, E159, E101, E223) whose members each also win at least one science.

A §3.5, 3.6; B F5, F6, F10, F12; E F6, F7; C F6.

F9. The fine-tunes did not touch the routers

GLM base vs HauhauCS: all 46 router matrices bit-identical (cosine 1.000000, difference zero). Qwen 35B base vs HauhauCS: overall prefill top-8 sets identical, W correlation 0.9977; the fine-tune shifts about 2 to 3% of routing slots through the residual stream. Any "the uncensored fine-tune rewired the experts" reading is out.

D F4; B F12.

F10. Cross-family: GLM shows the prefill half, and the generation half was never captured

On GLM prefill one default (E10) wins 12 of 20 domains with 15 prompts each; 5 distinct winners, Herfindahl 0.41. Same shape as Qwen, gentler, because sigmoid gating with the correction bias spreads load (the winner is selected on only 9% of slots and the top five defaults sit within 0.002 of each other, so 8 of 20 winners change identity between analysis choices). No GLM domain prompt was ever generated, so the second half of the claim has no cross-family test yet. The register runs have generation, and there winner identity changes between reading and writing in 15 to 17 of 20 prompts, but without domain labels that is a weak proxy.

D F1, F2.

4. Bugs found in the archive

122B: the 13-vs-15 prefill discrepancy is a last-layer capture artifact. llama.cpp evaluates the last layer for one token only during prefill. The routing-only script drops that layer (the 60 "missing layer" events); the generation script's split test lets it through with mostly generation rows counted as prefill, because the special-case check is hard-coded to layer 39, the 35B's last layer, and 122B has 48. The fingerprint is a uniform −0.0001 to −0.0002 drop across about 40 experts. The routing-only 13/20 is the clean reading. Keep it in the paper and disclose the separate capture.
122B: the "trimmed" generation track never trimmed. It scans for the 6-token BPE spelling of <|im_end|>, which never matches; real spill is the single <|endoftext|> token, present in all 60 cells. Mean clean answer 1,027 of 1,868 tokens, so about 45% of the 122B generation block is post-answer babble. The 18-distinct result was computed on that. It needs a proper re-trim, which needs tensors we no longer have. The 35B is unaffected: its trimmed and untrimmed tracks give the same winners and its trimmer fired where it should.
122B: E48 is a spill expert. The journal's "inner-state carrier" on 122B is rank 1 untrimmed and rank 53 (pooled) or 130 (softmax layers) inside the clean answer, rank 1 in the post-spill loop turns. Anything built on E48 should be re-read.
HVAC "180 prompts" are 18 prompts × 10 byte-identical repeats. The results file's n = 60 per register is really n = 6. The register effect there is large enough to survive (E114 generation W 0.0029 ± 0.0007 technical vs 0.0135 ± 0.0020 experience, no overlap), but every HVAC number should be quoted at n = 6.
Smaller. The token-balanced Jaccard uses top-8 sets, not top-15 as the paper implies (0.231 = 3/13). The philosophy-bias README misstates the design (F7). GLM's RESULTS.md says domain separation is "strongest early, L43 was noise"; the recompute shows two peaks of equal height. The 35B RESULTS.md token counts (2,404 / 110,412) differ slightly from the JSON cells (2,362 / 110,455).

6. What this means for the paper

  • Keep every headline number. All of them recompute.
  • Add F2 as the second result. The same-subject-same-crew finding from the packed prompts is stronger evidence of specialization than the winner tables, and it is already in the archive.
  • State F3 in Limitations with the numbers. Only four per-domain winners are stable at n = 3; name only those.
  • Add a register paragraph to Discussion (F4). The 60-prompt probe holds register constant, which is why its winners read as subject; the claim should be scoped to that. One sentence on the HVAC 0.50 → 0.00 result and the dialect null does it.
  • Fix the 122B disclosures: separate-capture prefill (bug 1), the trimmer no-op and the 45% spill in the generation block (bug 2). The 122B dispersion should be described as "answer plus spill, pending re-trim."
  • Small corrections: Jaccard top-8 not top-15; "attending to everything before it" is wrong for the DeltaNet layers; Hayashi et al. use the official FP8 checkpoint; define the 0.958 entropy as top-8 normalized; the 35B .npz holds per-layer summaries, not raw tensors.
  • Cite Wang, Hayou and Nalisnick (arXiv 2604.09780) for the prefill/generation premise on five other models, and say they did not test the domain question.
  • Causal subsection should say what F7 says: removable, not load-bearing.

7. What is missing

  • GLM domain generation captures. This is the one experiment that would make the claim cross-family. The prompts, the rig and the analysis exist; only the generation pass was never run.
  • A 10-plus-prompts-per-domain 35B run, so that per-domain winners can be named with confidence (P6) and the winner tables stop being the weakest layer.
  • A register-crossed domain probe: the same 20 subjects asked in an expository and a reflective voice, so the topic and register components of the generation winner can be separated on one design instead of inferred across three.
  • 122B raw router tensors, to re-trim the generation block and settle the last-layer artifact by row count. They were deleted on the box; a re-capture is the only route.
