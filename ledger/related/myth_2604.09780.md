## 2. Models, sizes, data

Models named in the paper (paper never states expert counts or top-k per model; it only gives the generic formulation "the top-k experts with the largest logit value"): gpt-oss-20b (most used), Qwen3-30B-A3B, ERNIE-4.5-21B-A3B (Base and PT), DeepSeek-V2-Lite, Moonlight-16B-A3B, Trinity-Mini-Base, Ling-mini / Ling-mini-base-2.0, plus dense controls (Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.2-3B, gemma-3-4b-pt, Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507). "five pre-trained models" in the abstract refers to the Fig. 1 / Fig. 5 set.

Data, verbatim:
- Fig. 1: "On the middle layer of 5 models, using 16 sequences from OpenWebText".
- Fig. 5: "100 sequences from OpenWebText and 100 from Nemotron-CC-Math (Mahabadi et al., 2025), evaluated both within and across datasets at the middle layer of five models".
- Fig. 6: "30 questions from HMMT February 2025 solved by 60 (reasoning) models, and study all (question, model, seed) combinations"; "averaged over 4 seeds and three MoEs" (Qwen3-30B-A3B, ERNIE-4.5-21B-A3B, gpt-oss-20b).
- Fig. 7 (Ling-mini): "query pairs that are matched along one axis but differ along another: (i) different concepts explained in the same style; (ii) the same concept explained in plain v.s mathematical language; (iii) AIME problems prompted to be solved in different languages." Figure labels: "Mathy explanation (GAN v.s. VAE)", "NUTS explanation (Plain v.s. Math language)", "Solving AIME (Chinese v.s. French)".
- Fig. 8: "we keep the 12 most-used experts (out of 32) in the last k layers, using usage frequency from a single hand-written paragraph" on gpt-oss-20b (so gpt-oss-20b has 32 experts; that is the only expert count stated).
- Fig. 9: "an HLE question (orange, 411 tokens) and a Wimbledon Wikipedia passage (purple, 2500 tokens) across four layers and five generation lengths". Prompts in App. C.1: "Summarize the following paragraph : [ history of wimbledon ]" and "Think step by step , answer the question : [ HLE question ]".
- Fig. 10: OpenWebText, Math, Code, Formal logic, duplicated "1× to 12×".

## 3. Definitions

Specialization (no single formal definition; the operative statement): "sequences from different domains tend to activate markedly different expert subsets". Their thesis: "hidden state similarity is both necessary and sufficient to explain expert usage similarity, and specialization is therefore an emergent property of the representation space, not of the routing architecture itself."

Expert usage: token level is the binary mask "m_t ∈ {0,1}^E is a binary vector denoting whether token t used the e-th expert." Sequence level is POOLED: "for each sequence of T tokens, we count the frequency of token visits for each expert and normalize it into a probability vector" (Eq. 13), compared by cosine (Eq. 14) or by "Overlap under top-P ... the smallest set of experts (sorted by decreasing frequency) whose cumulative probability mass exceeds P" with Jaccard (Eq. 19). Frequencies are unweighted visit counts, not routed weights.

Layers: mostly "the middle layer" (Figs. 1, 5) or all layers plotted (Figs. 2, 3, 6); Figs. 7, 9 show selected layers; Fig. 8 prunes "the last k layers".

## 4. PREFILL vs GENERATION (the key item)

They DO separate the two, and it is a central point of the paper. Settling sentences:

- Abstract: "prompt-level routing does not predict rollout-level routing".
- Intro: "semantically different prompts activate nearly identical experts during prefilling, but diverge during generation (Fig. 7)"; "sequences from unrelated domains can collapse to identical expert usage during prefilling (Figs.9, 10), yet the suppressed experts remain consequential once generation begins."
- Sec. 5.2: "We then track expert-usage similarity between paired queries' evolution from the prompt tokens through progressively longer rollouts. Fig. 7 reveals that during the prefill phase, all three query pairs show nearly identical expert usage. What happens during generation, however, depends on the query".
- Sec. 5.2 conclusion: "prefill-phase expert usage is not a reliable proxy for full generation usage pattern, as rollout content is unknown in advance, characterizing specialization requires observing complete trajectories."

Where the tokens come from, by experiment:
- Fig. 7: real rollouts from Ling-mini, tracked "from prompt through rollout. The dashed line marks the prompt boundary."
- Fig. 9: gpt-oss-20b rollouts, "five generation lengths for answering the HLE question" (600 to 5000 tokens).
- Fig. 8: mixed. Prompt-only corpora, then "HLE rollouts (On-policy)" and "AIME rollouts (Off-policy)"; so both self-generated and teacher-forced completions.
- Fig. 6: teacher-forced. The trajectories were "produced by different models" (60 MatharenA models) and are read through three MoEs; the MoE being measured did not generate most of them.
- Figs. 1, 2, 3, 5, 10: prefill of static text only (OpenWebText, Nemotron-CC-Math, etc.).

Important caveat for us: in Figs. 7 and 9 the "rollout" curves are cumulative over prompt plus generated tokens ("question + rollout", Fig. 14 caption; Fig. 9 x-axis "600 tokens ... 5000 tokens" with "Prompt (411 tokens)" as the first column). They do not report a generation-only block with the prompt removed. (Paraphrase: their generation measurements pool prefill and generation; ours never do.)

## 5. Domain organization during generation?

Domain is tested only on prefilled text. Fig. 5 and Fig. 10 compare OpenWebText vs Math vs Code vs Formal logic on static sequences; Fig. 5 finding: "cross-dataset pairs cluster at lower hidden-state similarity than intra-dataset pairs, offering a straightforward explanation for the specialist behavior observed in prior work: inputs from different domains tend to have different hidden-state distributions, and therefore engage different sets of experts." No numbers in text; Fig. 5 is a scatter.

During generation they never vary subject as the axis. Fig. 7 has one pair that differs in concept (GAN vs VAE) and two that differ in register or language; the finding is a per-pair story with no pooled statistic: "for some pairs the similarity remains high throughout, while for others it drops sharply as the rollout lengthens. Critically, which regime applies is not predictable from the prompt alone." Fig. 9 is one HLE question vs one Wimbledon passage: "During prefilling, the two unrelated sequences activate nearly identical experts in deeper layers. As the generation proceeds, their activation profiles diverge, with the split emerging earlier in later layers." Fig. 6, the one many-subject experiment, is across 30 math questions of the same domain, teacher-forced: "solutions from two different models to the same question show only ∼60% expert overlap on average, comparable to the overlap between solutions from the same model on different questions". No subject-identification test, no crew-turnover-at-boundary test.

## 6. Headline claim and evidence

"We show that, since MoE routers are linear maps, hidden state similarity is both necessary and sufficient to explain expert usage similarity, and specialization is therefore an emergent property of the representation space, not of the routing architecture itself."

Evidence: Prop. 1 (a data-aware Lipschitz bound on router logit distance) verified as a "triangle shape" of hidden-state distance vs logit distance on 16 OpenWebText sequences at the middle layer of 5 models (Fig. 1); the sequence-level version on 100+100 OpenWebText/Math sequences (Fig. 5); Prop. 2 that load balancing suppresses the shared hidden-state direction, with retained-energy curves on gpt-oss-20b vs Trinity-Mini (Fig. 2); and the three negative cases in Sec. 5 (Figs. 6, 7, 8-10).

## 7. Causal tests

One pruning test (Fig. 8, gpt-oss-20b): "we retain only the top-12 experts at the bottom layers and examine NLL changes across a variety of corpora. The first four columns of Fig. 8 shows that pruning experts in the last two layers has a negligible effect (<10% NLL increase) for prompt-only input across four different topics." And: "the last two columns of Fig. 8 confirm that the pruned experts are highly consequential for the model output's (conditional) likelihoods, both on- and off-policy." Caption: "Pruning up to 2 layers has a negligible effect (<10% NLL increase) on prompt-only inputs, indicating router collapse during prefilling. Last two columns: Adding completions makes the pruned experts consequential". No expert biasing, no steering, no removal of a domain's top experts with the answer checked.

## 8. Terms

- "router collapse": "during prefilling, semantically unrelated sequences can activate exactly the same experts"; "To probe how widespread this 'router collapse' is". Reuse, with credit; it names our flat-default prefill finding.
- "expert homogenization": "the failure mode in which all experts converge to redundant representations". Different thing (training failure); avoid conflating.
- "standing committee" / "shared committee experts": attributed to Wang et al. 2026 (2601.03425) and Hu et al. 2026; their gloss: "There exists a compact, domain-invariant expert that captures the majority of routing mass across all domains, with some peripheral experts showing domain sensitivity"; "shared committee experts likely reflect shared information in the hidden state space, potentially arising from shared syntax structure".
- "Context-Independent Specialization" (OpenMoE): they reject it, "which we believe arises from insufficient training".
- "dead router": hypothetical, "We did not observe such extreme 'dead router' patterns in open-sourced MoEs".
- "prefill" / "prefilling" and "rollout" / "generation": their words for our two blocks. They say "rollout" for generated tokens; we say "generation block". Either is fine; "rollout" signals RL-paper readers.
- "specialization collapse" (Qiu et al. small-batch effect). Avoid; it is a training-time term.
- They never use "generalist" or "polysemantic".

## 9. Agreement with our findings

1. Flat default in prefill, distinct winners in generation: AGREES. "semantically different prompts activate nearly identical experts during prefilling, but diverge during generation (Fig. 7)". Their mechanism differs (they attribute it to reasoning-model preamble tokens and depth-induced hidden-state correlation).
2. Crew tracks subject inside one answer, prefill rows fail the identification test: SILENT on the test itself; consistent in spirit: "prefill-phase expert usage is not a reliable proxy for full generation usage pattern".
3. Register moves crews as much as subject; dialect is a no-op: PARTLY AGREES / SILENT. Their pair (ii) "the same concept explained in plain v.s mathematical language" and (iii) language pairs show rollout divergence, but they do not quantify register vs subject and never test dialect.
4. Long coherent technical prompt shares the crew across prefill and generation: SILENT. Their long inputs (2500-token Wimbledon) are summarization prompts, and they report divergence, not sharing; no coherent-technical control.
5. Biasing leaders off leaves winners and answers intact: DISAGREES in emphasis, on a different model and test. "Adding completions makes the pruned experts consequential, with NLL increasing substantially" (12-of-32 kept in last k layers of gpt-oss-20b). They remove most experts and measure NLL; we remove four and check the answer. Not contradictory, but a reviewer will pair them.
6. Fine-tunes never touched the routers: SILENT. They do note removing the chat template "reduces the collapse" and that a non-reasoning PT model "do not observe a comparable collapse", which is about post-training changing hidden states, not routers.

## 10. What they did that we did not, and the reverse

They did: a theory (Props. 1, 2) tying routing to hidden-state geometry and load balancing; seven MoE families plus dense controls; hidden-state similarity measured alongside routing; a cross-model teacher-forced comparison over 30 HMMT problems with 4 seeds; an NLL pruning test on prompts vs completions; duplication and chat-template ablations; OOD confidence.

We did: a generation-only block that excludes prompt tokens; subject as an explicit 20-way axis at generation time with a held-out identification test and nulls (chance, position-matched, drift, shuffle); within-answer crew turnover at subject boundaries; register vs subject vs dialect on matched text; a targeted expert-bias causal test that checks the answer; a fine-tune vs base router identity check; 256-expert top-8 models.

## 11. Reviewer ammunition and the reply

- "Your generation-time subject structure is just hidden-state geometry; Wang et al. show routing is downstream of representation." Reply: agreed, and it is not in tension. Our claim is about what a prefill-only measurement misses, not about the router being the seat of specialization. Their Fig. 7 conclusion is our premise.
- "Wang et al. find prompt-level routing does not predict rollout routing, so your prefill-generation mismatch is known." Reply: they show it on three hand-picked pairs and one HLE/Wimbledon pair with cumulative (prompt plus rollout) pooling and no statistic; we separate the blocks, run 20 subjects, two prompt designs, and a held-out identification test with nulls.
- "Their pruning shows generation experts are load-bearing; your bias test says the leaders are removable." Reply: different operations. They drop 20 of 32 experts in whole layers and read NLL; we shift four leaders off in one model and read the answer. Removability of the top-4 does not contradict consequence of the bottom-20.
- "Router collapse in prefill is a gpt-oss reasoning-preamble artifact; they see no comparable collapse in ERNIE-PT." Reply: our flat default appears on a non-reasoning fine-tune and on the base checkpoint of Qwen3.5-35B-A3B with 256 experts, so it is not confined to a reasoning preamble; we should say so explicitly and cite their ERNIE-PT null as the contrast.
- "Frequency pooling vs mean routed weight": their crews are visit counts; ours are mean routed weight. Note it once so the overlap numbers are not compared directly.

## 12. Related-work sentences (draft)

Wang, Hayou, and Nalisnick (2026) argue that expert routing is a linear readout of hidden-state geometry, so expert usage similarity follows from hidden-state similarity rather than from the router itself. They also report that prompt-level routing does not predict rollout-level routing, with paired queries on Ling-mini showing near-identical expert usage during prefill and query-dependent divergence once generation begins, and that in gpt-oss-20b unrelated inputs collapse to the same deep-layer experts during prefill while the pruned experts become consequential for completions. Their evidence for the prefill-generation gap rests on a few hand-picked pairs and on similarity pooled over prompt and rollout together. We take their conclusion that characterizing specialization requires observing complete trajectories as a starting point, keep the prefill and generation blocks separate, and test whether generation-time routing is organized by subject across twenty domains with held-out identification and null controls.
